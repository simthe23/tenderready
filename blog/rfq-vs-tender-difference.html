RFQ vs Tender in South Africa: What Is the Difference and Which Should You Bid On?

By Amanani (Pty) Ltd · Last reviewed 13 October 2026

People use "tender" for everything the government buys, but there are different routes, and the route changes how much work a bid takes. For a small supplier, understanding the difference between an RFQ and a formal tender decides where to spend your time.

The short answer

An RFQ (request for quotation) is a simpler, faster process used for lower-value purchases. A tender (competitive bid) is a formal, longer process used for higher-value or more complex purchases. Which one applies depends on the value of the purchase and the rules of the buyer.

What decides which route is used

Public bodies are bound by supply chain management rules that set value thresholds. Below one threshold, a buyer may use petty cash or a quick informal price check. Above it, they must request written quotations from several suppliers. Above a higher threshold, they must run a formal competitive bid. The exact rand thresholds are set by National Treasury instructions for national and provincial bodies, and by municipal regulations and each entity's own policy, and they have been amended over time. Always check the current threshold with the entity or in its published supply chain management policy. Do not rely on a figure you saw in an old article.

What stays the same

  • You must be registered on the CSD and tax compliant.
  • You must return every required form, complete and signed.
  • Late submissions are not accepted.
  • Preference points can apply, so the cheapest price is not always the winner.

Why small suppliers often start with RFQs

  • Lower barrier. The paperwork is lighter, and you can learn the process on small orders.
  • Faster feedback. You find out sooner whether you won, so you improve sooner.
  • Lower cash risk. Smaller orders mean smaller stock outlay while you build a payment history.
  • Track record. Delivering on small orders builds the references that larger bids ask for.

When a tender makes sense

A tender can be worth it when the value justifies the effort, when you meet the technical and registration requirements, and when you can fund delivery. Construction and works tenders, for example, may require CIDB registration at a grade that covers the contract value, which many small suppliers do not have. Check this first, because it can rule you out before you start.

How to decide quickly

  1. Read the closing date and compliance requirements.
  2. Check whether you meet every mandatory requirement, including registrations and any compulsory briefing.
  3. Estimate your hours and your cash outlay.
  4. Check for red flags such as brand-locked specs.
  5. Decide go or no-go, and move on from the no-gos without guilt.
