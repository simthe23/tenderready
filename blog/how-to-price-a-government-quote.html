How to Price a Government Quote Without Guessing (or Losing Money)

By Amanani (Pty) Ltd · Last reviewed 13 October 2026

Pricing is where small suppliers either win at a loss or lose by a few rand. The way out is a method: confirm real costs, add everything it takes to deliver, apply a margin that fits the item, and check the result against the market. Here is the method, in order.

Step 1: Get a real cost for every line

Every line item needs a price from a source you could phone and order from today. That means a live product page, a published price list, or a written quote from a supplier. A remembered price or a "typical" price is a guess, and a guess in a government quote either loses the bid or wins it at a loss.

If you cannot confirm a price for a line, you have three honest options: keep looking, ask the supplier for a quote and see if it arrives before the closing date, or drop the RFQ. Submitting an estimate is the one option you should not take.

Step 2: Quote every line

A partial quote is usually non-responsive. If the RFQ has 20 lines and you can price 17, the quote is rejected or scored as incomplete at best. Before you invest further, check that you can source and price all lines. If a few cannot be found, widen the search to other suppliers before you give up.

Step 3: Work out the full landed cost

The supplier's price is only the start. Cost to the client's door includes:

  • The supplier price including VAT, because a non-VAT-registered business cannot claim it back.
  • Delivery from the supplier to you, and from you to the client.
  • Installation or labour, if any.
  • Packaging and handling.
  • Any card or bank fees.

Check whether a supplier quote says "ex works" or "collection". Missing the delivery line is one of the easiest ways to underprice a job.

Step 4: Apply a margin that fits the category

Margins are not the same across items. Commodity items many suppliers can source need a thin margin to be competitive. Specialised or hard-to-find items can carry more. Whatever percentage you use, make sure it covers three things beyond profit: the time you spent, the risk of price changes before payment, and the cost of waiting for payment.

Step 5: Check against street prices

Before you finalise, look up the public retail price of the same item. If your compliant, margin-inclusive price is higher than what a member of the public would pay in a shop, you are unlikely to win on price. You then have a choice: lower the margin, find a cheaper supplier, or skip the RFQ.

Step 6: Consider cash flow

Government and parastatal payment is commonly 30 days from invoice, and sometimes later in practice. If you have to buy stock upfront, you carry that gap. A win you cannot fund is not a win. Estimate whether the order's cash needs are within what your business can carry before you submit.

Step 7: Set a profit floor

Decide a minimum profit in rand for any order, and walk away below it. A 15% margin on a tiny order may not pay for your time, while the same margin on a large order may carry real risk. A rand floor protects you from jobs that look fine as percentages and are not worth doing.

Step 8: Second-pass check

Before submitting, recompute every line and the grand total from your cost sheet. Check that the declared price on the forms equals the quote total, in figures and in words. Errors at this step cost bids.

Keep a record

For each line, note the supplier name, contact, phone, quote reference and date. You need this if the RFQ is awarded, because it is how you place the order, and if there is a query, because it is how you show where the price came from.

The short version

  1. Real cost for every line.
  2. Quote every line.
  3. Landed cost, not supplier price.
  4. Margin that fits the category.
  5. Check against street prices.
  6. Check cash flow.
  7. Apply a rand profit floor.
  8. Recompute before submitting.
