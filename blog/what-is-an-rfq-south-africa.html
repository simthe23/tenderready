What Is an RFQ in South Africa? How Government Requests for Quotation Work

By Amanani (Pty) Ltd · Last reviewed 13 October 2026

An RFQ is a Request for Quotation. It is the way a government department, municipality, university or state-owned entity asks suppliers for a price on goods or services, usually smaller and simpler than a formal tender. For a small supplier, RFQs are often the most realistic way into public-sector work.

What an RFQ asks you to do

The buyer describes what they need, such as the items, quantities, specifications and delivery location, and asks you to send a priced quotation by a closing date and time. They compare the quotes they receive, check that each supplier is compliant, and award the work, often to the lowest compliant price or the best score under their preference point system.

What is inside a typical RFQ

  • The scope or item list. Line items with descriptions, quantities and units.
  • Specifications. Sizes, models, standards, and sometimes brand names.
  • The closing date, time and method. Email, an online portal, or a physical drop-box.
  • Compliance requirements. CSD registration, tax compliance, B-BBEE documents, and sometimes industry registrations such as CIDB for works.
  • Returnable documents. Standard bidding forms (SBD forms), declarations, and price schedules that must be completed and returned.
  • Delivery requirements and validity. When goods must arrive and how long your prices must stay valid.
  • Evaluation criteria. How the buyer will choose, including preference points.

Where RFQs come from

Entities publish RFQs on their own websites, in emails to suppliers on their lists, and on notice boards. Being registered on the CSD matters because many buyers draw their invitations from it. Check the procurement pages of the institutions you want to supply, and follow their notices regularly.

How to read an RFQ in five minutes

  1. Find the closing date, time and method first. A missed deadline cannot be fixed.
  2. List every compliance document they ask for, and check each one is current.
  3. Read the specifications for anything unusual, such as a model number or a feature that only one brand has.
  4. Check the delivery date against what a real supplier needs to deliver.
  5. Decide go or no-go before you price anything.

This screening step is the habit that saves the most time. Many RFQs are not worth quoting for a small supplier, and finding that out in five minutes is better than finding it out after six hours of pricing.

Warning signs

  • A specification that describes exactly one product.
  • A delivery window shorter than any supply chain could meet.
  • Any request for you to pay a fee or deposit to be considered. Genuine government procurement does not do this.
  • Contact details that look wrong, such as a personal email address claiming to be an official entity.
  • Quantities or specs that contradict each other in different parts of the document.

What makes a quote non-responsive

Buyers reject quotes before comparing price when documents are missing, forms are unsigned, the wrong submission method is used, or a compliance document has lapsed. A responsive quote prices every line, includes every returnable, and arrives on time through the right channel.
